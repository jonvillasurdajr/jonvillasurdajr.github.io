# A professional path through public service and healthcare

*By Jon Villasurda Jr. · August 1, 2026*

My professional path has centered on a consistent question: how can public systems translate policy and resources into services that work for people in practice?

I graduated from Owosso High School in 2003 before attending the University of Michigan. At Michigan, my education provided a foundation in psychology, public health, management, and policy. I was also a member of the Michigan Marching Band drumline during the 2004 and 2005 seasons—an experience that reinforced the value of preparation, coordination, and contributing to a larger organization.

My subsequent work in Michigan's healthcare and human-services environment made the practical dimensions of the central question much clearer. Programs operate within statutes, budgets, contracts, data systems, provider networks, and organizational responsibilities. They also operate in the lives of individuals and families who need services to be understandable and accessible.

Work involving Medicaid and behavioral health requires attention to both levels. Policy choices establish authority and direction. Implementation determines how those choices affect eligibility, access, payment, quality, reporting, and the daily work of providers and public agencies.

Over time, I have been fortunate to work with colleagues who bring different forms of expertise to these challenges. Lawyers, clinicians, financial specialists, analysts, program administrators, providers, advocates, and people with lived experience often see different parts of the same system. Better decisions emerge when those perspectives can be translated into a shared plan.

That has shaped how I approach projects today. I value clear objectives, practical governance, usable data, and direct communication. I also believe implementation plans should leave room for learning. Complex systems rarely behave exactly as expected, and responsible adjustment is part of the work.

Michigan remains an important part of my professional identity. It is where I studied, entered public service, developed much of my experience, and continue to live with my family. The work has also provided opportunities to learn from programs and partners in other states, each with its own structure, priorities, and local context.

The subject matter can be technical, but the underlying purpose is straightforward: build public programs that are coherent, sustainable, accountable, and useful to the people they are intended to serve. That purpose continues to guide my work.

An early [Metro Times profile, “The young and uninsured,”](https://www.metrotimes.com/news/the-young-and-uninsured-2194448/) described my interest in healthcare access while I was completing my Master of Public Health degree in 2009. The roles and projects have changed since then, but the underlying interest in making healthcare systems work better has remained consistent.
